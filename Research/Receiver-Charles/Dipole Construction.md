Ideally, a dipole should consist of:
- Antenna wire elements
- Coaxial cable connector
- Insulators
	- Three total
		- One for each open end of the elements
		- One for the center including holding the connector

# Why insulators?
When you hang up an antenna, especially in our desired way of being in a tree, there must be a solid connection between the rope and the wire. This is so any wind acting on it doesn't unfurl them and result in the antenna being disconnected from the rope, and thus falling from the tree.
In order to form a solid connection with the rope and the wire, the only method would be to tie them together. This means, every time you put the antenna up in the tree, the antenna length would be modified slightly. 

# What is an insulator?
An insulator is basically a non-conducting material that has a slot to tie the antenna to, and another slot separated by the non-conducting material to tie the rope. This allows for the antenna length to stay consistent and for consistent SWR measurements when put up, for antenna wire insulation in case the wire used is steel (insulators are also used for holding up towers, so thus steel wire), and for the weakest link in the chain to be antenna wire or the ropes instead of the connection.

## What is an insulator made of?
Insulators are usually made of plastic, pvc, or some type of ceramic. In the antenna build we did last semester as a 40m dipole, the insulators we made (specifically, for the center insulator), we 3d printed an insulator out of PLA that worked just fine.

# Why 3 insulators?
As said before, you need two insulators for each end of the antenna, since this antenna in specific is a dipole. This means there are two equal antenna wire lengths on both sides, requiring both sides to be attached, and for an insulator to be used on both sides. 
The third insulator is located in the middle of the antenna, where each antenna wire connects to the connector, either the center pin or the shielding.
## Why a third one?
Without the third insulator, the tension of the antenna being in the tree, with winds and such, all of the tension put onto the antenna would be put onto the weakest link. Usually, the weakest link in this system is the connection from the antenna wire to the connector in the center. These connections are mostly soldered, and while solder connections are strong, they are very non-flexible, and thus easy to break upon any loads. Not to mention, the center pins of many soldered connectors are not designed at all to withstand any load laterally or horizontally.
Thus, the reason for a third one insulator is to practically tie the antenna wire to some material. This transfers all the tension and strain to the material itself, and the wire ends connecting to the actual connector result in having no strain. This allows for the weakest link in the system to either be the insulators (which, if built decently, will never fail), the antenna wire, or the rope. Like said before, this allows for practically no strain to be on the connector itself.
Additionally, it provides a very nice and solid place to put the connector, so it's not just blowing in the wind.


The choice of connector itself has very limited effect on the signal. Some connectors have less loss, but the difference in loss is so minimal that the only difference would be in systems trying to minimize every fraction of decibel loss in the system. 
Another consideration when choosing a connector is the easability to connect and disconnect coaxial systems.
- Usual connectors that are used are UHF (PL-259 and SO-259), BNC (like TV cable), and N-type.
	- The easiest connectors to disconnect and connect are the BNC connectors, as they are practically push-and-turn to connect fully. UHF and N-type connectors, on the other hand, act basically as screws and bolts which provide extremely strong connections, but take much longer to connect as there's lots of threads and moving parts.
